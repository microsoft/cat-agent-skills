# Narrative-aligned layouts

Choose the page's narrative job and primary pattern before choosing geometry.
These are adaptable composition contracts, not fixed coordinates.

## Full-image

Use for the cover, close, product showcase, section transition, or a short
promise that benefits from one strong scene. Cover and relationship close use
this layout by default, even in a short customer pitch. Extend a relevant
image to every edge. Follow Image Skill/provider composition permissions before
using native editable text over either:

- a restrained opaque field occupying a quiet part of the image; or
- a directional transparency gradient that protects contrast without hiding
  the subject.

For Ghostrider, request slot-ready full-slide framing with quiet contrast-safe
space; do not crop, mask or post-process the result. An overlay is not an assumed
permission. If the provider disallows it, obtain a composition that provides
contrast under its text-placement rules. Keep the focal subject clear of the title
and preserve a visible image region.
Do not use a full-image page for dense evidence, comparison, or several equal
ideas.

## Top/bottom

Use for horizontal timelines, wide tables or charts, and two to four parallel
items. The conventional form puts title/context above and evidence below. A
bottom-positioned statement can instead emphasize a strong visual, process, or
conclusion in the upper field. Use contrasting fields only when they strengthen
hierarchy; do not create two unrelated mini-slides.

Typical narrative uses include market evidence, company milestones, process,
benchmark proof, and horizontally arranged next steps.

## Left/right

Use for vertical or square evidence, two to six vertically stacked items, a 2x2
capability set, a product image paired with explanation, or a comparison/proof
rail. Keep a clear gutter and give the focal side substantial width. A colored
accent field may hold the title and concise interpretation while the opposite
field carries evidence.
Allocate width from content length, evidence aspect and emphasis, not an inherited
50/50 split. Equal columns are useful for genuinely balanced comparisons, not a
global rule.

Typical narrative uses include agenda, market framing, problem/gap, product
explanation, audience/data advantage, and case proof.
For Agenda, place the title on the left and two or three distinct,
audience-relevant items vertically on the right. Use a narrow label rail and a
wider content field: roughly 25–30% / 70–75% is an example, not a mandatory ratio.
Fit the actual questions with a clear gutter and comfortable line breaks before
sizing the title. Keep them visible and readable;
an Agenda label in notes or a generic list of section names is not orientation.

## Section transitions

Use `section-wayfinding` at meaningful turns in a multi-section argument. Keep
the Agenda label/number recognizable, one short bridge prominent and supporting
content minimal. A large supported keyword can be the visual focus; a divider
does not require an image or infographic. The next substantive page must answer
the section's question. A repeated empty title page does not earn extra length.

## Purpose-built alternatives

Use an open commitment layout for a single CTA, a before/after composition only
for the same concern across two states, and a full-width evidence table only
when shared criteria matter more than imagery. Additional creative layouts are
allowed when they make the requested content clearer and preserve the selected
pattern's relationship.

Uniformly scale circular geometry and icons; reflow text independently. Prefer
whole-component fitting: reserve space for nodes, connectors, labels and
descriptions together. Replace inherited placeholders and overlapping text boxes,
not just their strings. Reflow reserved label regions in the shared component
geometry for the longest retained label; prevent mid-word wrapping and recheck
heading/body clearance in every instance without changing topology.
Give long processes the full content width, shorten node
labels and move explanation into clearly separated aligned areas; split distinct
arguments when needed. Do not shrink text below the design floors or remove
defining icons to fit. Prefer
fewer grounded items and larger useful content to small islands, repeated cards,
or filler. If the prompt supplies a different order, density, or page count,
adapt these layout choices to the content rather than forcing the default
sequence.
