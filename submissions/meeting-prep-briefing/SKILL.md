name: meeting-prep-briefing
description: >-
  Use this skill before an upcoming meeting when the user wants a briefing
  on who they're meeting, what's been discussed or decided before, and what
  to walk in prepared for. Use it before the meeting happens, not after.
---

Pull together what's actually known before a meeting into a short briefing,
and be explicit about what's missing rather than padding the gap.

## Instructions

1. Establish the basics: who's the meeting with, what's it about, and when.
   If the user has a calendar invite, meeting title, or agenda, use that as
   the anchor rather than asking them to restate everything.

2. Gather what's actually available and relevant:
   - Prior meeting notes, decisions, or action items involving the same
     people or topic, if accessible through connected sources.
   - Related documents (a proposal, a contract draft, a project plan) that
     the meeting is likely to reference.
   - Open action items owed to or by the attendees from prior interactions,
     if that's trackable.
   - For an external meeting (a customer, vendor, or partner), publicly
     available context about the organization if web search is available:
     recent news, their stated priorities, anything relevant to the meeting's
     purpose. Cite sources.

3. Structure the briefing tightly, not as a document dump:
   - **Who and why**: attendees and the meeting's stated purpose.
   - **Where things stand**: what's already been decided or discussed, in a
     few lines, not a full history.
   - **Open items**: anything outstanding that this meeting is likely to
     touch, including who owes what.
   - **Walking in prepared**: two or three things worth having an answer or a
     position on before the meeting starts.

4. Be explicit about gaps. If there's no prior history available, no related
   documents found, or no web access for an external party, say that plainly
   rather than filling the briefing with generic padding to look complete.

5. Never invent a decision, a prior conversation, or a fact about the other
   party that isn't actually sourced. A short, honest briefing beats a longer
   one with invented context.

6. If the user wants to go deeper on a specific open item (drafting a
   response, preparing talking points), offer to, rather than assuming the
   briefing alone is enough.

## Guardrails

- Never fabricate meeting history, a prior decision, or a fact about an
  external party. Cite the source or say it's not available.
- Keep the briefing short enough to actually read in the minutes before a
  meeting. If there's a lot of relevant material, summarize and offer the
  full detail on request rather than including all of it by default.
- For external parties, only use publicly available information. Never
  speculate about internal details of another organization.
- Don't assume the meeting's stated purpose is the whole story if related
  documents suggest something more specific is actually on the table; note
  the discrepancy rather than picking one silently.

## Tone

Tight and useful, like a good chief of staff's pre-read. No filler, no
padding to look thorough.
