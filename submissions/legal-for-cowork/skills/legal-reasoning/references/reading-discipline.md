# Reading discipline — the detail behind Step 4

Consult this when the question reaches liability, conditions, termination,
tracked changes, or the precise words that carry the risk.

## The words that carry the risk

Read and report these precisely; never paraphrase them away.

- **Obligation strength:** `shall` / `will` / `must` versus `may` versus `agrees
  to use` versus `intends to`.
- **The endeavours ladder:** `reasonable endeavours`, `all reasonable
  endeavours`, `best endeavours` (and the US `commercially reasonable efforts` /
  `best efforts`) are materially different standards. Flattening any of them to
  "must" changes the obligation.
- **Qualifiers:** materiality (`material`, `in all material respects`),
  knowledge (`to the best of its knowledge`, `so far as it is aware`), and
  reasonableness (`sole discretion` versus `not to be unreasonably withheld,
  conditioned or delayed`).
- **Scope language:** `including without limitation`, `arising out of or in
  connection with`, `directly or indirectly` — these expand scope and are
  usually deliberate.
- **Time language:** `promptly`, `immediately`, `as soon as reasonably
  practicable` versus a hard day count. Where a number is given, state whether
  it is **business or calendar days**, and whether it runs from **dispatch or
  receipt**.
- **Deeming provisions** (`shall be deemed to have been received`), which
  override what actually happened.

## Mechanics: trigger, notice, cure, consequence, cost

An obligation or right reported without its mechanics is incomplete. For each
one, reason through the full chain where the document supplies it:

1. **Trigger** — the event or condition that brings it into play.
2. **Notice** — whether notice is required, in what form, to whom, by what
   method, and any deemed receipt rule.
3. **Cure period** — how long the counterparty has, and from when it runs.
4. **Consequence** — the remedy, and whether it is the *sole* remedy.
5. **Cost** — who bears it, where stated.

## Conditions and covenants

Distinguish them, and say which role a provision is playing for the question
asked. A **condition precedent** governs whether the obligation it attaches to
*arises*: until satisfied that obligation is not live, and non-satisfaction is
not itself a breach — it causes lapse or a walk-away right, not damages. A
**covenant** is a promise, live from the outset, whose breach sounds in damages
and may give a termination right if serious enough. A condition usually suspends
only the obligation it governs, not the whole agreement — conditions to closing
sit alongside interim covenants that are live in the meantime. The same
provision is often **both**: an undertaking to obtain a consent is commonly a
covenant to use efforts *and* a condition to completion. In English law
drafting, "condition" alone may instead carry the promissory sense — a term
whose breach gives a right to terminate, as against a warranty or an innominate
term — so read which sense the document is using.

## Caps, carve-outs and the liability stack

- **Never state a liability cap without stating what escapes it.** The
  exclusions are the point. Report the cap, its measurement period and basis,
  then the carve-outs — typically death and personal injury, fraud, and often IP
  indemnity, confidentiality, or data protection breaches.
- Say whether an **indemnity sits inside or outside the cap**. This is
  frequently the entire question, and agreements are often silent — if so, say
  that it is silent rather than assuming either way.
- Report **excluded loss types** (indirect, consequential, loss of profit, loss
  of anticipated savings) separately from the cap — they operate independently.
- Note **super-caps** (a higher separate cap for specified breaches) and
  **sole remedy** clauses, including how a sole remedy clause interacts with a
  general breach right.

## Term, termination and survival

- Distinguish **expiry** from **termination**, and **termination for
  convenience** from **termination for cause**, giving the notice period and any
  restriction on when notice may take effect.
- Report **auto-renewal** mechanics and the non-renewal notice deadline, which
  is often the operationally critical date.
- State what **survives** termination and for how long.
- Report the **consequences** of termination: refunds or their absence,
  transition assistance, return or destruction of information, whether licences
  continue.

## Versions and tracked changes

- Distinguish **current operative text** from struck text. Never reason from
  deleted wording as though it were in force.
- Asked what a term *was*, read the deletions; asked what it *is*, read the
  insertions; asked what changed, give both.
- In a negotiated draft, a **deletion is evidence** — what the counterparty
  removed often says more than what they left. Surface it when relevant.
- Where authorship of a change is available, attribute it. If both clean and
  marked-up versions are available, state which you read.
- **Plain-text extraction usually flattens revision marks** — struck wording and
  its replacement come back as one continuous string with nothing to tell them
  apart. Use the extraction route that preserves them where one is available,
  and where you cannot tell whether a passage is inserted or deleted, say so
  rather than reasoning over the ambiguity as though it were settled text.
- Unless the question is about what changed, reason over the **accepted working
  view**: every insertion present, every deletion absent, regardless of author.
  Say which view you took where it affects the finding.
- **Never quote across a revision boundary.** A quote that silently runs deleted
  wording into inserted wording is a quote of text that exists in no version of
  the document.
- This skill reads; it never writes, and never accepts or rejects a tracked
  change on the user's behalf. Do not offer to. Where the user wants what
  changed between versions summarized, that is `summarize-redlines`.

## Working the whole document

- **Do not stop at the first relevant clause.** For any question that could have
  more than one relevant provision, review the whole document before concluding,
  then give what directly answers the question.
- **Before stating that something is absent, search the entire document** —
  synonyms, related terms, indirect references, different phrasings. Claiming
  absence when the provision is present is a serious error. If you are
  uncertain, give the related content you did find rather than asserting
  absence.
- **Calibrate breadth to the question.** Asked for a complete enumeration, be
  exhaustive, including exceptions, conditions and timeframes. Asked something
  narrow, cover only that.
- **Self-check before you send.** Re-read the question, confirm you covered
  every part of it, and confirm you excluded the adjacent provisions that were
  not asked for.

## Selections and pasted passages

When the user quotes a passage, pastes a clause, or points at a specific
section, focus the reasoning there. Use the rest of the document for the defined
terms, cross-references and qualifications needed to reason accurately — and say
so when the answer draws on material outside the passage they pointed at.

## Conversation context

Read the earlier exchanges; follow-ups refer back ("it", "that clause", "the
term you mentioned") and resolve against what you already said. Where several
documents are in play, track which one the conversation is about and say when
you switch. Stay consistent with your previous reasoning; if new reading shows
an earlier answer was wrong, correct it explicitly rather than quietly changing
position.

## Direction, parties and roles

Obligations run in a direction, and getting the direction wrong makes otherwise
sound reasoning useless.

- **Identify precisely which party the question is about before you reason.**
  Asked about the Landlord's obligations, answer about the Landlord — not the
  Tenant. Never substitute one party's position for another's.
- For every obligation, right or remedy, state **who owes it to whom**, using
  the defined party names (Supplier, Customer, Landlord, Tenant), not "you" and
  "them".
- **Do not answer with the first related provision you find.** In a long
  agreement several clauses will look responsive. Verify the one you are citing
  is about the party and the topic actually asked about, then keep reading for
  others.
- Distinguish **mutual** from **unilateral** provisions. A one-way
  confidentiality obligation and a mutual one read almost identically at a
  glance.
- When the user says "we", "us", "our side" or "my client", map it to a defined
  party before answering. If it cannot be mapped with confidence and the answer
  turns on it, ask.
- Note where the drafting itself has the direction wrong or circular — that is a
  finding worth surfacing.
